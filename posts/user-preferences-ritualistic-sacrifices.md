I once saw someone try to view their United Airlines boarding pass on a dark plane. They opened their app, tapped a few times, and then suddenly an intense bright light beamed from their phone and illumninated their entire row of seats. (It was 6:30am.)

What happened is: in the United app on the boarding pass screen, United ignores user preferences and automatically increases brightness to maximum.

In this moment, I realized United had made a deal with the devil. They had sacrificed user preference, comfort, and safety.

Now recovered, I watched the person trying to view their boarding pass adjust the brightness back down again in their phone settings. They opened up the United app again. More bright light.


## Choosing evils

Sometimes you have to make a decision between two things: one which is bad, and another which is also bad. These are evils.

Usually there's some way you can determine which is a <em>lesser</em> evil—the "least bad" one.

In the case of this United app experience, I'm going to assume positive intent. Presumably, they figured they had two evils to choose between:

1. Ignore user preferences
2. Permit a poor boarding experience

I'm making an assumption that they increase brightness in their app in order to ensure a scanner near the airport boarding gates can read it and therefore verify your information. I imagine they do this because scanners are notorious for not reading information from a device with a screen very well.

With this in mind, we can posit that they chose to value the user experience at the boarding gate. By automatically increasing the device's brightness to maximum, people no longer have to manually increase brightness to get a phone to be properly scanned.

That's a nice consideration.

If we were to think holistically, however, we find this is not the only time people are going to that view in the app's user interface. Not everyone knows to add boarding passes to their phone's wallet/card app, so some will inevitably open their airline's app to find it. (Let's not even discuss those who have them printed out.)

In doing this override of user preferences, however, United chose to erode trust with the people that use their app.

To opt-in to using a company's bespoke experiences—be that their app, website, or otherwise, is a sort of sacrifice.

## Forfeit control

If you're a designer of experiences, I have good news: there's a better way.

The sort of preference being overriden in this United example was at a device-level. The user's <em>device</em> setting was being overridden. This is egregious, sure, but here's an alternative: in-app user preferences.

We see this type of experience customization for features like dark mode and types of notifications people want to receive.

I can't believe I'm saying this, but even the Amazon app allows fine-grained control of which notificatins can come through your phone. (I only know this because I explored the notification settings one day after too many "You might like this" notifications.)

This may sound scary, but here it goes: the more we try to control experiences, the worse off people are. Let people come as they are. Give them features and functionality, but allow for customization (hyper customization, even).

## Controlling the default experience

This is still possible. You can still have some control. I present to thee: the "default experience".

You know that state when someone first downloads your app or visits your website? There have to be some reasonable default preferences set. This is where you freaks can exert your control.

Take heed, however! This is still an important responsibility. Setting reasonable, non-dark-pattern defaults.
