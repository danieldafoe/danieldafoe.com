A while ago I saw a post on BlueSky where someone mentioned they were writing a disclaimer for an LLM chatbot. The disclaimer would inform people that output from the chatbot couldn't be relied upon and should be fact-checked.

[LLM-driven agents make mistakes][llm-misinformation-bbc]. Sure. But when did we start adding disclaimers for things on the web that can give incorrect information?

How often do you read "Disclaimer: May not taste as advertised" for recipes you find online? Or how about "Disclaimer: Some reviews may not be by humans" when checking out reviews of products?

We as a society have decided to subject LLMs to heavier scrutiny.

## The dawn of misinformation

You don't have to travel far in the era of the internet to come across misinformation. From Instagram influencers and TikTok educators to YouTubers and even recipe-sharers, there's no shortage of people who confidently pass along information without verifying its truthiness.

Moreover, the history of misinformation goes further back than the internet—as far as Ancient Rome. People were spreading falsities long before we had wires in the ocean connecting the world.

So what's with the double standard? Why is AI subject to heavier scrutiny?

Let's explore some possibilities.


## Possibility: LLMs propagate biases

When asked for a negative aspect of AI, a faculty member at Virginia Tech said, "as these models are trained on vast amounts of internet text, they might unknowingly propagate biases present in their training data." (Gonsalves et al, 2023). 

This makes sense. If LLMs are trained on a gigantic dataset consisting of 90% male research papers vs 10% female, they're going to have an inherent bias toward referencing research by male researchers. If majority of those papers pointed at male interviewers as being more likely to hire men than women, when an LLM is predicting the most logical next word in its output, your darn right it's more likely to regurgitate that. 

Similarly, however, this homogeneity of data also impacts human biases. Our own "training data", such as individual experiences and the people around us, shape who we think we can trust, which features belong to which genders, what our idea of beauty is, and even what values we should respect.

Therefore, it's fair to say our biases can be a catalyst for the sharing of misinformation. Ever heard of [confirmation bias][confirmation-bias]?

We're going to have to do better than bias propagation to justify our scrutiny of LLMs.

## Possibility: LLMs pose legal risk

This, to me, is the most likely culprit of extra scrutiny. Companies are scared.

I imagine the likelihood of someone initiating a lawsuit is higher for a company whose chatbot explains differences in credit card benefits than influencer Cindy-Lou Hoo telling you to get only 2 hours sleep per day. This would explain why chatbots are under more scrutiny than everyday people.

But why is that the case? Why aren't there lawsuits against fitness influencers who explain how to do exercises properly? Someone tries it, hurts themselves, and lashes out at the owner of a 30-second clip they saw online. At what point is the person consuming the information at fault? I know no one wants to take responsibility for their mistakes, but could it be that someone didn't do the exercise correctly? Could be equally possible that someone took the word of a word prediction machine (i.e., LLM) without verifying its accuracy?

## Possibility: Humans are speciesist

Yes, OK, LLMs aren't a <em>species</em>.

The point here is that humans tout themselves as superior to all other entities that we perceive as "thinking".

We don't have to do much digging to uncover this trait. Most people deem it acceptable to kill animals for food despite us not needing to, most people think it's acceptable to test experimental drugs on non-human animals first, and humans are quick to demolish the habitats of other animals due to our extreme selfishness and greed.

## Universal disclaimers

Let's face it: the web is a trove of misinformation. Maybe we should be putting an asterisk beside every piece of content across the web that imparts information with the implied feature that it's factual.

Is recent and abundant skepticism for AI due to the now multiple generations of people being online-by-default? AI gets a lot of hate, but I don't entirely think it's all warranted. Sure, it's not entirely accurate all the time. Do the makers of these tools pitch 100% accuracy as a feature? No.


## AI Isn't the Enemy

As I've hopefully demonstrated, the animosity people feel for AI is not so well-placed. We believe AI is to blame for the spread of misinformation despite our own kind perpetuating it for millennia.

Who is really to blame here? The tool built by humans—Superspreaders of Misinformation™—or humans themselves?

(Humans is the answer.)
